1.

A snapshot is not a trajectory

The reverse SDE and its probability flow ODE share time marginals under exact conditions. Their individual paths differ.

Question : what is actually equal?

A time marginal describes the position of a randomly chosen sample at one fixed time. A path law also includes dependence across times. The SDE and probability flow ODE can match the former while differing in the latter. Even starting from the same position, Brownian increments create a new source of randomness along the SDE route; the ODE route is fixed by its initial value and velocity field.

How to read the two equations

The score is the spatial gradient of log density at a fixed time. It describes local relative density increase, not the direction to a particular target sample. In the reverse SDE, the drift and Brownian term jointly determine the evolving distribution. The probability flow ODE reproduces that distribution by changing the drift as well as removing the stochastic term; simply deleting noise from the SDE would give a different equation.

Read the histograms as finite-sample estimates of a common law, not identical point sets. Then follow one sample : agreement of distributions does not imply agreement of trajectories. The claim requires the exact score, the corresponding initial law and well-posed continuous-time dynamics.

2.

From path tangents to a velocity field

A geometric view of Flow Matching : each endpoint pair gives a path tangent; conditional averaging gives one velocity at each position and time.

Question : what does the mean field preserve?

Conditional averaging preserves the probability transport needed for the evolving marginal density. It does not assign each particle its original target endpoint. The grey arrows are representative compatible tangents, and their opacity encodes posterior weight. Integrating the red mean gives a new family of trajectories. The score identity computes that same mean field under the stated Gaussian assumptions, rather than recovering the endpoint labels.

Why condition on position and time?

An endpoint pair specifies both an interpolated position and its tangent velocity. Different pairs can be compatible with the same observed position and time, so position alone does not reveal a unique conditional tangent. The expectation averages these possibilities using their posterior probabilities given the observation. It is not an equal average over target clusters.

Move the observation point between clusters and inspect the changing arrow weights. The red mean and blue score-based ring coincide because both evaluate the same exact field. Under squared-error Flow Matching, this conditional mean is the population regression target. The display computes it analytically; it does not show a fitted network or a training result.

In the interpolation, a controls the Gaussian-source contribution and b the target-endpoint contribution; the dots denote time derivatives. The bridge equation rewrites the posterior average as a linear term plus a score correction. Its Gaussian-source and independence assumptions matter : it is not an identity for every possible coupling or interpolation.

The linear term and score term sum to the conditional-mean velocity. With a Gaussian source, independent endpoints and a compatible schedule, this is the probability flow ODE written in generation time. Equal fields give equal ODE trajectories from the same initial point.

Averaging does not preserve each original endpoint pairing. Conditional paths and the mean-field ODE share marginals, not particle identities. Here the schedule is linear; only 0.05 ≤ t ≤ 0.95 is simulated to avoid singular endpoint coefficients.

3.

When the score is no longer exact

Introduce the same horizontal score bias into both samplers. Then refine the integration grid to separate sensitivity to score error from numerical effects.

Question : which source of discrepancy changed?

The bias changes the score used by the samplers, while the time grid changes the numerical approximation to their dynamics. Initial samples and the underlying Brownian realization are shared, so changing the step count does not draw an unrelated SDE experiment. Compare the reference and current rows at the same time. Early or late bias tests when the dynamics are sensitive; it does not isolate a universal advantage of stochastic or deterministic sampling.

A controlled comparison, not a ranking

First set bias to zero and change the step count : the field is exact, but finite sampling and discretization remain. Then hold the step count fixed and introduce a bias. This changes the field itself; increasing the number of steps cannot generally repair that change. The same score perturbation enters the SDE and ODE with different coefficients, so it need not produce the same distribution error.

The CDF metric asks how much the fraction of samples to the left of a threshold differs from the analytic reference, taking the largest gap over thresholds. A small value only supports horizontal-coordinate agreement. The 1,024-step reference is also numerical and contains sampling variability; one seed does not establish that either sampler is universally more robust.

What stays the same?

SDE ↔ ODE : the same marginals, different paths. Gaussian FM ↔ the matching probability flow ODE : the same exact velocity. Approximate scores : neither equality is automatic.
