When optimal denoising
amplifies perturbations.

Posterior covariance, denoiser Jacobians, and the limits of minimum-MSE reconstruction.

A Bayes-optimal denoiser minimizes reconstruction error. Does it also suppress small changes in the observation? The answer is controlled by posterior uncertainty.

Starting point: DDPM/NCSN prediction identities · extension: local sensitivity
01 / ESTIMATOR GEOMETRY

From Tweedie’s identity to a covariance Jacobian

Fix a noise level: y = ax₀ + σε with a > 0 and ε ∼ 𝒩(0, I). Write D(y) = E[x₀ | y], ε*(y) = E[ε | y], and s*(y) = ∇ log p(y). Starting from the lecture’s prediction identities, differentiate D with respect to its observation.

D(y) = [y − σε*(y)] / a = [y + σ²s*(y)] / a

The local response of the optimal denoiser is determined by its posterior covariance:

JD(y) = (a/σ²) Cov(x0 | y) = [I + σ²∇²y log p(y)] / a

For a unit direction v, the directional gain vᵀJ_Dv equals (a/σ²) Var(vᵀx₀ | y). An unresolved direction in the posterior can therefore become an amplified direction in the output. Minimum MSE does not imply a contraction.

‖JD(y)‖2 = (a/σ²) λmax(Cov(x0 | y))
‖JD(y)‖2 > 1 ⇔ λmax(Cov(x0 | y)) > σ²/a

Derivation from the posterior score

∇y log p(x0 | y) = (a/σ²)[x0 − D(y)]
∂Di/∂yj = E[x0,i ∂yⱼ log p(x0 | y) | y]
　= (a/σ²)[E[x0,ix0,j | y] − Di(y)Dj(y)]

Differentiate under the posterior integral. Gaussian smoothing and finite conditional second moments justify the identity under the usual integrability conditions. The result applies to a general prior; the next experiment uses a finite prior so every term can be checked exactly.

Extension of lecture 2’s Tweedie formula; derivative identity: Dytso, Poor & Shamai.

D′ is a local quantity. A finite h can cross the steep transition region, so its secant gain need not equal D′(y). Reducing h tests the linear approximation directly.

Large local gain can be a rare event

At y = 0, D′(0) = a/σ² grows as noise decreases. But y = 0 also lies between the clean modes and can become extremely unlikely. A large worst-case derivative is not the same as a large average reconstruction error.

Bayes optimality, stability, and valid samples are different

For the same posterior, compare three decisions: the conditional mean, a MAP estimate, and an independent posterior draw. They optimize different criteria. At the symmetric boundary, the mean is unsupported even though it has the smallest conditional MSE.

A candidate estimate u has two costs: its distance from the conditional mean, and the uncertainty that remains after observing y. Only the first can be reduced by choosing u.

The predictor is a controlled simulation: ε̂ = ε + ηz, with independent standard-normal z. The slider changes its error, not a trained network. η = 0 uses the true injected noise as an ideal reference.

Why the same error matters more at high noise

If the prediction misses the noise by δ, the position error is multiplied by σₜ/aₜ. More noise means less signal, so a small prediction error can leave a visibly misaligned puzzle.

Lecture 1, “One model, three parameterizations” (slide 60/85), converts noise prediction into x-prediction. Here aₜ denotes the lecture’s ᾱₜ, and σₜ = √(1 − aₜ²). All formulas apply coordinate by coordinate.

Define SNR = aₜ²/σₜ². Then the squared reconstruction error is the squared noise-prediction error divided by SNR. Equal noise-prediction error at two noise levels need not imply equal reconstruction error.

Extending the identity to an imperfect score

ŝ(y) = s*(y) + e(y)
D̂(y) − D(y) = (σ²/a)e(y)

A predictor can be accurate at a point but vary rapidly nearby. Controlling reconstruction error and controlling local sensitivity therefore require different information. The puzzle does not assume or measure a learned J_e.

05 / SCOPE & REFERENCES

What is calculated, and what is assumed

Analytic probability model

The covariance–Jacobian identity assumes Gaussian corruption with scalar a > 0 and finite posterior second moments. The scalar experiment uses an equally weighted two-point prior. Its mean, derivatives, conditional risks, and amplification interval are analytic; the interval probability uses 512-panel Simpson quadrature.

Training and implementation

All estimators are computed analytically; no network is trained. The photographic puzzle is a separate synthetic predictor-error experiment. Local denoiser expansion does not by itself establish instability of a multi-step diffusion sampler.

Course connections

Lecture 1 supplies noise/x prediction and conditional-mean optimality. Lecture 2 supplies Tweedie’s formula and the score/noise identity. Differentiating these identities gives the local-sensitivity analysis developed here; it is an extension, not a claim that this whole derivation appeared in the lectures.
