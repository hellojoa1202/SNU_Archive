INTERACTIVE COURSE NOTES

Three approximations
of a diffusion sampler.

Prior, score, time grid: what a sampler replaces, measured one at a time.

The reverse-time SDE and the probability flow ODE are exact when run from the true terminal law with the true score in continuous time. A sampler has none of the three: it starts from a Gaussian prior, follows a learned score, and takes discrete steps. The lecture notes name these as the only approximations. These notes build a toy in which every other quantity is known in closed form, so that each substitution can be switched on alone and its effect on the generated distribution measured.

Toy model: pdata is an equal mixture of 48 Gaussians of width τ = 0.06 on an 8 × 6 grid, the forward process is dx = dw, and the score, the marginals, the cell masses and the KL to the prior are all computed in closed form or by quadrature. No neural network is trained. The picture can be read as an array of optical traps whose light intensity is pt; that reading is stated where it helps and its limits are listed at the end.

1. THE FORWARD PROCESS

The forward SDE and its marginals

The forward process is the variance-exploding instance dx = dw, with f = 0 and g = 1, so σ²(t) = t. Under an affine drift the perturbation kernel is Gaussian, pt(xt | x0) = N(x0, t I), and the marginal is the data mixture with every component widened by t. The density solves the heat equation ∂tp = ½ Δp; the samples below are one realisation of the SDE whose law it is.

pt(xt | x0) = N(xt ; x0, [σ²(t) − σ²(0)] I)

Computing the kernel: NCSN (variance exploding) · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 18 · here σ²(t) = t.

pt(xt) = ∫ pt(xt | x0) pdata(x0) dx0

Marginal densities · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 21 · with pdata a mixture of 48 Gaussians of variance τ², the integral is the same mixture with variance τ² + t; the Fokker–Planck equation with f = 0 is the heat equation ∂tp = ½ Δp (p. 34, 51).

2. GENERATION

Reverse-time SDE and probability flow ODE share the marginals

Both generative equations are certified the same way: write the Fokker–Planck equation of the candidate, check that the forward marginals solve it, and let uniqueness do the rest. They are the two ends of a one-parameter family. For every η ≥ 0 the process below passes through the same marginals, because the extra ½ η² g² of score in the drift cancels exactly the extra ½ η² g² Δp that the noise adds to the Fokker–Planck equation. η = 1 is the reverse-time SDE, η = 0 the probability flow ODE.

dx̄(t) = [ f(x̄(t), t) − g(t)² ∇ log pt(x̄(t)) ] dt + g(t) dw̄(t),    x̄(T) ∼ pT

Reverse-time SDE · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 63.

dx̃(t)/dt = f(x̃(t), t) − ½ g(t)² ∇ log pt(x̃(t))

Probability flow ODE · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 73.

dx̄(t) = [ f − (1 + η²)/2 g² ∇ log pt ] dt + η g dw̄(t),    η ≥ 0

One marginal family, many processes · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 78 · η = 1 is the reverse-time SDE, η = 0 the probability flow ODE.

The marginals agree at every time; the trajectories do not. Solutions of the ODE never cross, so the map from x1 to x0 is a bijection; the SDE's endpoint is not a function of its start. Both KS distances include finite-sample and discretization effects; 1024 exact draws give about 0.03.

Everything above used the true p1, the true score and a fine grid.A sampler has none of them. The next section replaces them one at a time.

3. THE THREE APPROXIMATIONS

Prior, score, discretization: one substitution at a time

With f = 0 and g = 1 one backward step of the family is a single line, and a sampler changes exactly three things in it: the law of xT, the score it evaluates, and the step h. The lecture lists the substitutions, pprior for pT, sϕ for ∇ log pt, a grid for the continuum, and notes that they are the only three. Here each is switched on alone while the other two stay exact.

dx̄ = [ f(x̄, t) − g²(t) sϕ×(x̄, t) ] dt + g(t) dw̄,    dx̃/dt = f(x̃, t) − ½ g²(t) sϕ×(x̃, t),    x̄(T), x̃(T) ∼ pprior

From the oracle to the model · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 86 · the prior for pT, the model for the score, and, next, the discretization of the equation: “three approximations, and they are the only three”.

xt−Δt ← xt − [ f(xt, t) − g²(t) sϕ×(xt, t) ] Δt + g(t) √Δt z

Euler–Maruyama step of the reverse-time SDE · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 87 · the Euler step of the probability flow ODE drops the noise and halves the score term; Heun adds a second evaluation per step (appendix p. 16).

DKL(pT ‖ N(0, P(T) I)) ≤ ½ SNR(T) E‖x0‖²,    SNR(T) = E(0→T)² / P(T)

The residue is the signal-to-noise ratio; from the kernels to the marginal · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 23, 26 · here E ≡ 1 and P(T) = T, so SNR(T) = 1 / T.

The score error is not something the toy can take from a trained network, so two controlled errors stand in for sϕ× − ∇ log pt: a smoothing δ ≥ 0, which evaluates the exact score at the later time t + δ, and a constant bias b. The score a sampler sees is then ∇ log pt+δ(x) + b. This substitute is defined on this page; denoising score matching (p. 83–84) is why the learned score is a regression estimate in the first place.

2048 samples, 600 drawn; every metric uses all of them. Mode mass error compares the fraction of samples nearest to each of the 48 centres with the exact mass of p₀ in that cell: it asks where the mass went. Spread is the root-mean-square distance to the nearest centre relative to its exact value: it asks how tightly the samples gathered. Small grey numbers are the values for 2048 exact draws from p₀, the floor of each metric.

  • Prior. The lecture bounds the mismatch by the signal-to-noise ratio, KL(pT ‖ N(0, P(T) I)) ≤ ½ SNR(T) E‖x₀‖² (PDF p. 23, 26). Here SNR(T) = 1 / T; the bound is loose by an order of magnitude but has the right trend. The ODE is a bijection and carries whatever is wrong at T to t = 0; the SDE keeps injecting noise and pulling back with the full score, and forgets its start.
  • Score. A constant bias moves mass between modes and shifts the whole distribution; smoothing keeps the mode masses and widens every mode. The family multiplies the score, and hence the bias, by (1 + η²)/2, so in this toy the SDE is more sensitive to a constant bias than the ODE. The usual robustness argument for stochastic sampling concerns errors that fluctuate and average out, which a constant bias does not.
  • Discretization. The score is stiff: ‖∇ log pt‖ grows like 1 / (τ² + t), so the last steps near t = 0 decide the result. At equal evaluations Euler on the ODE is the most accurate up to about 128; Heun overshoots on coarse grids and pays off only from 256 on; Euler–Maruyama needs √h ≪ τ in its last step or its own noise widens the modes.

The budget is not additive. A bias that moves mass to the wrong mode cannot be repaired by more steps, and a coarse grid cannot be repaired by a longer forward time. The two substitutions that move mass (prior, bias) and the two that widen it (smoothing, grid) separate in the two metrics.

5. THE SAME THREE, ELSEWHERE

Flow matching is a probability flow ODE

For a Gaussian source the field that flow matching learns is a score in other letters: the conditional velocity is an affine function of the conditional score, and averaging over the posterior turns it into the marginal score. With the matched schedule αs = bT−s, σs = aT−s, the flow-matching ODE and the probability flow ODE of that diffusion coincide in distribution on (0, T).

vt(x) = (ḃt / bt) x + at² (ḃt/bt − ȧt/at) ∇ log pt(x)

And the marginal velocity is a marginal score · Flow-based notes, PDF p. 64 · identification on the open interval p. 69, 74.

  • The prior is no longer an approximation: the interpolant reaches pprior exactly at t = 0 because b₀ = 0. The price is paid by the matched diffusion, whose coefficients diverge at that endpoint; a diffusion cannot reach N(0, I) in finite time.
  • The score error becomes a velocity error. Conditional flow matching regresses on ȧtx₀ + ḃtxT, and its minimiser is the marginal velocity by the same conditional-expectation argument that makes denoising score matching work.
  • The discretization is unchanged. Straight conditional paths do not make the marginal trajectories straight; the solver still follows a curve.

Scope and the optical-trap reading

The toy can be read as a holographic array of optical traps: light intensity pt, particles in a fluid, Brownian motion when the light is off, a restoring force when it is on, a defocused beam for δ and a sideways flow for b. The reading is exact for one Gaussian beam, whose gradient force is linear in the displacement, and loose for the array, where the real force is ∝ ∇I while the sampler follows ∇ log pt. The schedule τ² + t is a mathematical choice. Bias and smoothing stand in for the unknown error of a trained network because their effects can be predicted and checked, not because they are typical. The time grid is uniform; non-uniform spacing is a standard remedy for the stiffness in 3 and lies outside the lecture material.

Model and sources

Toy: 48 Gaussians, τ = 0.06, pitch 0.22, forward dx = dw. Closed forms for pt, ∇ log pt, cell masses and the x-marginal; KL by 140 × 140 quadrature. Samplers: the η-family with Euler–Maruyama for η > 0 and Euler or Heun for η = 0, seeded so every run replays. Tests (node --test) check the score against finite differences, the heat equation, the KL bound, cell masses against Monte Carlo, and marginal alignment of the family.
