01 / RECONSTRUCTION & GENERATION

Reconstruction begins with a representation

An autoencoder compresses x into a representation z and decodes it back. A VAE gives this representation a probability model, allowing new latent samples to be decoded into data.

xdata
→
qθ(z | x)encoder
→
zlatent variable
→
pϕ(x | z)decoder
→
x′output
ℒELBO = Eqθ(z|x)[log pϕ(x | z)] − DKL(qθ(z | x) ‖ p(z))

The first term rewards reconstruction; the KL term controls how the encoded distribution differs from the latent prior. With a Gaussian decoder of fixed variance, the reconstruction term becomes a squared-error objective up to scaling and constants.

DDPM fixes the forward encoder to a Gaussian noising chain and learns the reverse process. Its prediction target can be the added noise, the clean signal, or an equivalent parameterization.

x0 → x1 → ⋯ → xT　q: fixed noising
xT → xT−1 → ⋯ → x0　pϕ: learned reversal

The shared question is what a squared-error predictor returns when several originals could explain the same representation. Sections 02–04 make that question exactly computable.

02 / FORWARD NOISING

Two possible originals, one noisy observation

Use a small data distribution: x₀ = −1 or +1, each with probability 1/2. Adding Gaussian noise turns these two points into overlapping distributions.

y = atx0 + σtε,　ε ∼ 𝒩(0, 1)

pt(y) = ½ 𝒩(y; −at, σt²) + ½ 𝒩(y; at, σt²)

Notation follows lecture 1: aₜ is its ᾱₜ, the signal amplitude. In DDPM, σₜ = √(1 − aₜ²). NCSN keeps aₜ = 1 and conditions its score network on the noise level. Here both can be inspected over the same σ range.

The optimum predicts the conditional average of the injected noise, not necessarily the particular noise that produced one observation. The reconstructed output is consequently a conditional mean.

Derive the noise–score identity
∂y log p(y | x0) = −(y − atx0)/σt² = −ε/σt
∂y log pt(y) = E[∂y log p(y | x0) | y] = −E[ε | y]/σt

Differentiating the Gaussian likelihood gives its conditional score. Averaging it under p(x₀ | y) yields the marginal score. Rearranging E[ε | y] = (y − aₜE[x₀ | y])/σₜ gives the clean estimate, also known as Tweedie’s formula for scaled Gaussian corruption.

This identity connects prediction targets at the same corruption level. DDPM and NCSN still differ in their noise schedules, training weights, and sampling algorithms; NCSN originally uses annealed Langevin dynamics.

04 / CONDITIONAL MEAN

The best reconstruction may be an average

At y = 0, the two originals are equally plausible. Their conditional mean is 0, even though 0 never appears in the clean data. A squared-error optimum need not be a valid data sample.

m(y) = E[x0 | y] = tanh(aty/σt²)

A candidate estimate u has two costs: its distance from the conditional mean, and the uncertainty that remains after observing y. Only the first can be reduced by choosing u.

E[(x0 − u)² | y] = (u − m(y))² + Var(x0 | y)
Var(x0 | y) = 1 − m(y)²

Why the conditional mean minimizes MSE
x0 − u = (x0 − m) + (m − u)
E[(x0 − m)(m − u) | y] = (m − u)E[x0 − m | y] = 0

The cross term vanishes because m is the conditional mean. The remaining variance is independent of u. This is the same least-squares argument behind the Gaussian VAE decoder and the optimal noise predictor.

A puzzle of noise prediction

The previous experiments use optimal conditional predictions. This puzzle isolates a different question: how does a specified error in noise prediction affect the reconstructed positions?

The predictor is a controlled simulation: ε̂ = ε + ηz, with independent standard-normal z. The slider changes its error, not a trained network. η = 0 uses the true injected noise as an ideal reference.

Why the same error matters more at high noise

If the prediction misses the noise by δ, the position error is multiplied by σₜ/aₜ. More noise means less signal, so a small prediction error can leave a visibly misaligned puzzle.

06 / MODEL & REFERENCES

What is calculated, and what is assumed

Analytic probability model

Sections 02–04 use P(x₀ = ±1) = 1/2 and Gaussian corruption. Densities, posterior probabilities, conditional means, and scores are calculated exactly. The sample strip uses 80 seeded draws; its finite counts need not equal the analytic density.

Training and implementation

No neural network is trained. Exact optima make the equations inspectable in the browser. The photo puzzle uses a separate, explicitly simulated noise predictor. Its image is fixed; only piece positions enter the calculation.

Course connections

Lecture 1: autoencoders, VAE ELBO, conditional-mean decoding, DDPM, and noise/x parameterizations. Lecture 2: denoising score matching, Tweedie’s formula, NCSN, and the score–noise relation.
