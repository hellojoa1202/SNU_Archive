INTERACTIVE COURSE NOTES

Three approximations
of a diffusion sampler.

Where does the error of a diffusion sampler come from, and which part of the sampler is responsible?

In the lectures the reverse-time SDE and the probability flow ODE were proved exact under three conditions: the process starts from the true terminal law pT, it uses the true score ∇ log pt, and it runs in continuous time. A practical sampler satisfies none of them. It starts from a Gaussian prior, it evaluates a trained network in place of the score, and it takes a finite number of steps. The lecture notes point out that these are the only three approximations involved. That raises a question the slides do not answer: how much does each one cost, and does the answer depend on whether we sample with the SDE or the ODE? These notes answer it on a toy problem small enough that everything except the three substitutions can be computed exactly, so that each one can be switched on by itself and its effect on the final distribution measured rather than guessed.

The toy: pdata is an equal mixture of 48 Gaussians of width τ = 0.06 placed on an 8 × 6 grid, and the forward process is the variance-exploding SDE dx = dw. For this choice the marginal density, its score, the probability mass around each mode and the KL divergence to the prior are all available in closed form or by quadrature, so no network is trained and nothing is estimated that could be computed. If a physical picture helps, the same setup is an array of optical traps whose light intensity is pt; section 2 shows it on an optical bench, and the end of the page states where the reading stops being exact.

1. THE FORWARD PROCESS

The forward SDE and its marginals

Everything a sampler does is an attempt to run the forward process backwards, so it is worth being precise about what the forward process destroys. We use the variance-exploding instance of the forward SDE from the lecture, dx = dw with f = 0 and g = 1, so that σ²(t) = t. Because the drift is affine, the perturbation kernel is a Gaussian centred at the starting point, and the marginal pt is obtained by averaging that kernel over the data.

p_t(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\big(x_t ;\, x_0,\ [\,\htmlData{link=time}{\sigma^2(t) - \sigma^2(0)}\,]\, I\big)

Computing the kernel: NCSN (variance exploding) · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 18 · here σ²(t) = t.

p_t(x_t) = \htmlData{link=mixture}{\int p_t(x_t \mid x_0)\, p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)\, dx_0}

Marginal densities · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 21 · for a mixture of Gaussians of variance τ² the integral is the same mixture with variance τ² + t; the Fokker–Planck equation with f = 0 is the heat equation ∂tp = ½ Δp (p. 34, 51).

For our data the integral can be done by hand: a Gaussian convolved with a Gaussian is a Gaussian, so pt is the same 48-component mixture with every component widened from τ² to τ² + t. The 48 modes are 0.22 apart, so they stop being distinguishable once the width √(τ² + t) is comparable to half that distance, that is, around t ≈ 0.01, and by t ≈ 0.05 the density is a single blob. From then on the forward process is only making a broad Gaussian broader. The signal-to-noise ratio of the lecture, SNR(t) = E(0→t)² / P(t) = 1 / t here, keeps track of how much information about x0 survives in xt; it never reaches zero at finite time, which is the first of the three approximations in waiting.

The forward process is easy to write down and impossible to invert sample by sample.What can be inverted is the family of marginals. The next section shows the two processes the lecture constructs for that purpose, and why they agree on distributions while disagreeing on paths.

2. GENERATION

Reverse-time SDE and probability flow ODE share the marginals

The lecture certified both generative equations with the same argument: write down the Fokker–Planck equation of the candidate process, check that the forward marginals pt solve it, and conclude from uniqueness that the candidate passes through every pt. The reverse-time SDE does this with the full score and fresh noise; the probability flow ODE does it with half the score and no noise at all. Neither statement says anything about individual trajectories, and the slide titled “matching marginals, not trajectories” insists on the distinction.

d\bar x(t) = \big[\, f(\bar x(t), t) - \htmlData{link=score}{g(t)^2\, \nabla \log p_t(\bar x(t))} \,\big]\, dt + \htmlData{link=randomness}{g(t)\, d\bar w(t)}, \qquad \bar x(T) \sim p_T

Reverse-time SDE · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 63.

\frac{d\tilde x(t)}{dt} = f(\tilde x(t), t) - \htmlData{link=score}{\frac{1}{2}\, g(t)^2\, \nabla \log p_t(\tilde x(t))}

Probability flow ODE · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 73.

The two are not separate inventions but the ends of one line. For any η ≥ 0 the process below also solves the forward Fokker–Planck equation when run backwards: the extra ½ η² g² of score in the drift produces exactly the term ½ η² g² Δp that the extra noise adds, so the two cancel for every η. Setting η = 1 gives the reverse SDE and η = 0 the probability flow ODE; values in between, and above one, are equally legitimate samplers. Which one to use is a design decision, not a mathematical one, and the third section will show that it matters once the ingredients stop being exact.

d\bar x(t) = \Big[\, f - \htmlData{link=eta}{\frac{1+\eta^2}{2}}\, g^2\, \htmlData{link=score}{\nabla \log p_t} \,\Big]\, dt + \htmlData{link=randomness}{\eta\, g\, d\bar w(t)}, \qquad \eta \ge 0

One marginal family, many processes · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 78 · η = 1 is the reverse-time SDE, η = 0 the probability flow ODE.

The same equation on an optical bench: beads in an array of light traps

AUTO + MANUAL

A physical picture. Optical tweezers hold a micron-sized bead in water at the focus of a laser beam. Near the focus the light pulls the bead back with a force proportional to its distance from the centre, and the water adds Brownian kicks, so the bead obeys an overdamped Langevin equation: a drift down a potential, plus noise. The reverse-time SDE has exactly this form if the potential is Ut = −log pt. Near a mode, −log pt is a harmonic well of stiffness 1 / (τ² + t), so each of the 48 modes is one tweezer and lowering t stiffens every trap at once. In the equation above, the drift term is the force of the light, the noise term is the thermal bath, and η is the temperature of that bath: η = 0 switches the bath off and the bead slides down its well, η = 1 is a bead in water. The picture is loose in two places, so that it is not taken too far. Between traps the force here is ∇ log pt, which always hands a bead on to a neighbour, whereas real beams have dark gaps; and in a lab the temperature can be set independently of the stiffness, while here the extra drift ½ η² g² ∇ log pt is tied to the noise so that every η keeps the same marginals.

Everything above used the true p1, the true score and a fine grid.A sampler has none of them. The lecture slide “from the oracle to the model” lists what replaces each one, and the next section replaces them one at a time, keeping the other two exact, so that the damage can be attributed.

3. THE THREE APPROXIMATIONS

Prior, score, discretization: one substitution at a time

Sampling substitutes the prior for pT, because pT is a mixture over the whole dataset; it substitutes the trained network sϕ× for ∇ log pt, because the marginal score cannot be evaluated; and it substitutes a grid for the continuum, because a computer integrates step by step. The lecture writes the two generative equations with these replacements made and remarks that the three are the only approximations involved, so if a sample is wrong, one of the three is to blame. The experiment below is built to find out which.

\begin{aligned} d\bar x &= \big[\, f(\bar x, t) - g^2(t)\, \htmlData{link=score-term}{s_{\phi^\times}(\bar x, t)} \,\big]\, dt + g(t)\, d\bar w, \qquad & \htmlData{link=start}{\bar x(T) \sim p_{\mathrm{prior}}} \\[2pt] \frac{d\tilde x}{dt} &= f(\tilde x, t) - \frac{1}{2}\, g^2(t)\, \htmlData{link=score-term}{s_{\phi^\times}(\tilde x, t)}, & \htmlData{link=start}{\tilde x(T) \sim p_{\mathrm{prior}}} \end{aligned}

From the oracle to the model · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 86 · “Three approximations, and they are the only three.”

x_{t-\Delta t} \leftarrow x_t - \big[\, f(x_t, t) - g^2(t)\, \htmlData{link=score-term}{s_{\phi^\times}(x_t, t)} \,\big]\, \htmlData{link=grid}{\Delta t} + g(t)\, \htmlData{link=grid}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\, z

Euler–Maruyama step of the reverse-time SDE · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 87 · the Euler step of the probability flow ODE drops the noise and halves the score term; Heun adds a second evaluation per step (appendix p. 16).

\htmlData{link=start}{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_T \,\|\, \mathcal N(0, P(T)\, I)\big) \le \frac{1}{2}\, \mathrm{SNR}(T)\, \mathbb E\lVert x_0\rVert^2}, \qquad \mathrm{SNR}(T) = \frac{E(0 \to T)^2}{P(T)}

The residue is the signal-to-noise ratio; from the kernels to the marginal · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 23, 26 · here E ≡ 1 and P(T) = T, so SNR(T) = 1 / T.

The first and third substitutions are easy to reproduce exactly as a sampler makes them: start from N(0, T I) instead of pT, and use Δt = T / N instead of the continuum. The second is not, because the toy has no trained network and the error of a real one is not something we can write down. Two controlled errors stand in for sϕ× − ∇ log pt. A smoothing δ ≥ 0 evaluates the exact score at the later time t + δ, which is what a regression that averages over nearby inputs would do; it is the realistic one, and it bites hardest near t = 0 where the true score changes fastest. A constant bias b adds the same vector everywhere; it is the simplest systematic error and the one whose effect on the SDE and the ODE differs most. The sampler then sees ∇ log pt+δ(x) + b. This substitute is defined on this page, not in the lecture; the lecture's contribution is the reason a learned score is a regression estimate at all, namely denoising score matching (p. 83–84).

  • Prior. Starting from N(0, T I) is wrong by exactly the KL divergence that the lecture bounds through the signal-to-noise ratio. In the toy the bound is 0.2 nats at T = 1 while the true divergence is 0.02, so the bound is loose by an order of magnitude, but it has the right dependence on T. What happens to that initial error afterwards depends on η. The ODE is a bijection: whatever mass sits in the wrong place at time T is transported, deformed but conserved, to time 0, and at T = 0.25 about a fifth of it lands in the wrong mode. The SDE keeps injecting noise and pulling back with the full score, so by the time it reaches t = 0 it has largely forgotten where it started, and the same experiment shows half the error.
  • Score. The two errors leave different fingerprints. A constant bias moves the whole distribution sideways, so mass crosses from one mode to the next and the first metric rises, while the width of each mode is unchanged. Smoothing leaves the mode masses alone and widens every mode, because near t = 0 the sampler is told the density is broader than it is. There is also a result that may be surprising: in this toy the SDE is more sensitive to a constant bias than the ODE, not less. The family multiplies the score, and therefore the bias, by (1 + η²)/2, which is 1 at η = 1 and ½ at η = 0; the extra noise cannot average out an error that points the same way at every step. The folklore that stochastic sampling is robust to score error is about errors that fluctuate, and a bias is the opposite case.
  • Discretization. The score is stiff: ‖∇ log pt‖ grows like 1 / (τ² + t), so a uniform grid wastes most of its steps where nothing happens and arrives at the last few steps, where the modes form, with a step that is too large. With few evaluations Euler on the ODE is the most accurate of the three methods, and Heun, despite its higher order, is the worst, because its predictor overshoots the narrow modes; Heun only pays off from about 256 evaluations on. Euler–Maruyama has an additional requirement, √Δt ≪ τ in its last step, otherwise the noise it injects at the very end is wider than the modes it is trying to form.

Three substitutions, two kinds of damage.The prior and a biased score move mass between modes; a smoothed score and a coarse grid widen the modes. The next section sweeps each knob across its range to make the comparison quantitative.

Prior · mass in the wrong mode against T

The ODE inherits the prior mismatch in full and loses it only as T grows; the SDE forgets it during the run and sits at the floor for every T.

Score bias · mass in the wrong mode against bx

Linear in the bias for both samplers, and steeper for the SDE by the factor (1 + η²)/2. At bx = 1 the distribution has moved by more than one mode spacing.

Score smoothing · mode width against δ

Width grows steadily with δ and the two samplers agree; the mass metric (not shown) stays at the floor until δ ≈ 0.03, so smoothing widens modes without moving mass between them.

Discretization · mode width against evaluations

Heun is worst below 32 evaluations, over-contracts around 128 and is best from 256 on; Euler–Maruyama approaches the exact width slowly from above because its last step needs √Δt ≪ τ.

The budget is not additive. A bias that has moved mass to the wrong mode cannot be repaired by taking more steps, and a grid that is too coarse at the end cannot be repaired by running the forward process longer. This is also why the two metrics were chosen: the first answers where the mass went, the second how tightly it gathered, and the three substitutions fall on different sides of that line.

5. THE SAME THREE, ELSEWHERE

Flow matching is a probability flow ODE

The final part of the flow-based lecture shows that flow matching, which never mentions a score, learns one anyway when the source is Gaussian. Conditioned on the data endpoint, the interpolant is Gaussian, its conditional score is available in closed form, and the conditional velocity turns out to be an affine function of that score. Averaging over the posterior turns the conditional score into the marginal score, so the learned field is a drift plus a multiple of ∇ log pt. Choosing the diffusion whose schedule matches the interpolant, αs = bT−s and σs = aT−s, makes the match exact: on the open interval (0, T) the flow-matching ODE and that diffusion's probability flow ODE have the same solutions in distribution.

v_t(x) = \htmlData{link=fm-drift}{\frac{\dot b_t}{b_t}\, x} + \htmlData{link=fm-score}{a_t^2 \Big( \frac{\dot b_t}{b_t} - \frac{\dot a_t}{a_t} \Big) \nabla \log p_t(x)}

And the marginal velocity is a marginal score · Flow-based notes, PDF p. 64 · identification on the open interval p. 69, 74.

For the question of these notes this has a direct consequence: a flow-matching sampler is a probability flow ODE sampler, so it makes the same three substitutions, in slightly different clothes.

  • The prior stops being an approximation. The interpolant reaches pprior exactly at t = 0 because b₀ = 0, so there is no mismatch to bound. The lecture shows where the cost went: the matched diffusion's coefficients diverge at that endpoint, because no diffusion with regular coefficients reaches N(0, I) in finite time.
  • The score error becomes a velocity error. Conditional flow matching regresses on ȧtx₀ + ḃtxT, and its minimiser is the marginal velocity by the same conditional-expectation argument that made denoising score matching work; a trained field misses that minimiser in ways our bias and smoothing imitate.
  • The discretization is unchanged. The lecture's Gaussian example has straight conditional paths and curved marginal trajectories, with st² = (1 − t)² + t², so the solver still follows a curve and a coarse grid still costs what it cost here.

Scope and the optical-trap reading

If a physical picture is wanted, the toy is a holographic array of optical traps: light intensity pt, particles in a fluid, Brownian motion while the light is off, a restoring force when it is on, a defocused beam for δ and a sideways flow for b. The reading is exact for a single Gaussian beam, whose gradient force is linear in the displacement, and loose for the array, where the physical force is proportional to ∇I while the sampler follows ∇ log pt. The schedule τ² + t is a mathematical choice. Bias and smoothing stand in for the unknown error of a trained network because their effects can be predicted and checked, not because they are typical. The time grid is uniform; non-uniform spacing is a standard remedy for the stiffness seen in section 3 and lies outside the lecture material.

Model and sources
