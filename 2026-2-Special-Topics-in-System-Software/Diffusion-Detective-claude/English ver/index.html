INTERACTIVE COURSE NOTES

Light Traps.

Three approximations of a diffusion sampler, measured one at a time.

A focused laser pulls a small particle towards the bright spot: an optical trap. A hologram can make many bright spots at once, and particles that were drifting freely in the fluid gather into the pattern. Read the light intensity as a probability density and the pull as its score, and this is a diffusion sampler. The reverse-time SDE and the probability flow ODE are exact on paper; a sampler replaces three things it does not have, the true terminal law, the true score, and continuous time. Here everything else is known in closed form, so each replacement can be switched on alone and its effect on where the particles end up can be seen.

The toy: 48 trap sites on an 8 × 6 array; the intensity field at time t is pt; the force a particle feels is ∇ log pt. For a single Gaussian beam that is the familiar linear restoring force of an optical trap. Contours show the light; dots are particles; the bars count how many particles each trap captured against the exact share of probability mass. 1. LASER OFF · THE FORWARD PROCESS

Particles diffuse, the pattern blurs

Switch the laser off and each particle performs Brownian motion in the fluid: dx = dw, the variance-exploding forward SDE with zero drift and unit diffusion. The particles start in 48 tight clusters of width τ = 0.06, one per trap site. Because the drift is affine, the density at time t is the same mixture of 48 Gaussians with every component widened by t, in closed form. No simulation is needed to know the law; the dots are one realisation of it.

pt(x) = (1/K) Σk N(x ; μk, (τ² + t) I),    K = 48

Perturbation kernel and marginal density · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 18, 21 · with f = 0, g = 1 the Fokker–Planck equation is the heat equation ∂tp = ½ Δp (p. 34, 51).

2. LASER ON · GENERATION

Pulling them back: one family of samplers

Now turn the light on and sharpen it over time, so that at each instant the intensity is pt with t running down to zero. There is not one way to drive the particles but a one-parameter family. For every η ≥ 0 the process below has the same marginals as the forward process: the extra score in the drift cancels exactly the extra diffusion the noise adds to the Fokker–Planck equation. η = 1 is the reverse-time SDE, particles pulled through their own thermal jitter; η = 0 is the probability flow ODE, particles dragged along the field with no jitter at all.

dx̄ = [ f − (1 + η²)/2 g² ∇ log pt(x̄) ] dt + η g dw̄

One marginal family, many processes · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 78 · reverse-time SDE p. 63, probability flow ODE p. 73.

3. THE THREE APPROXIMATIONS

Three substitutions, one at a time

With f = 0 and g = 1 one backward step of the family is a single line. A sampler changes three things in it: where the particles start, which field they feel, and how large the step h is, that is, how often the field is updated. The lecture notes name these as the only three approximations, and this toy keeps every other quantity exact so that each can be measured alone.

xT ∼ N(0, T I),    x ← x + (1 + η²)/2 · s̃(x, t) · h + η √h z,   z ∼ N(0, I)

From the oracle to the model · Score SDE notes, PDF p. 86 (the three substitutions) · Euler–Maruyama step p. 87.

s̃(x, t) = ∇ log pt + δ(x) + b

Defined on this page. A learned score is a regression estimate (denoising score matching, p. 83–84) whose error is unknown; in its place, two controlled errors: a defocus δ that reads the field of a blurrier time, and a drift b that pushes every particle the same way.

600 of the 2048 particles are drawn; every metric uses all of them. Capture error compares the fraction of particles nearest to each trap with the exact mass of p₀ in that cell: it asks where the mass went. Spread is the root-mean-square distance to the nearest site, relative to the exact value: it asks how tightly the particles gathered. Small grey numbers are the values for 2048 exact draws from p₀, the floor of each metric.

  • Start. The lecture bounds the mismatch by the signal-to-noise ratio: KL(pT ‖ N(0, P(T) I)) ≤ ½ SNR(T) E‖x₀‖² (PDF p. 23, 26). Here SNR(T) = 1 / T; the bound is loose by an order of magnitude but has the right trend. The ODE is a bijection and carries whatever is wrong at T to t = 0; the SDE keeps adding jitter and pulling back, which lets it forget where the particles were released.
  • Field. A drift moves mass between traps and shifts the whole pattern sideways; a defocus keeps the captures right and widens every cluster. The family multiplies the field, and hence the drift, by (1 + η²)/2, so in this toy the SDE is more sensitive to a constant drift than the ODE. The usual robustness argument for stochastic sampling concerns errors that fluctuate and average out, which a constant drift does not.
  • Steps. The field is stiff: ‖∇ log pt‖ grows like 1 / (τ² + t), so the last pulses near t = 0 are the ones that matter. At equal evaluations Euler on the ODE is the most accurate up to about 128; Heun overshoots the traps on coarse grids and pays off only from 256 on; Euler–Maruyama needs √h ≪ τ in its last step or its own jitter widens the clusters.

The budget is not additive. A drift that moves mass to the wrong trap cannot be repaired by more pulses, and coarse pulses cannot be repaired by a longer laser-off time. The two substitutions that move mass (start, drift) and the two that widen it (defocus, pulses) separate cleanly in the two metrics.

5. THE SAME THREE, ELSEWHERE

What flow matching changes, and what it does not

Flow matching never writes a score, yet for a Gaussian source the field it learns is one: the conditional velocity is an affine function of the conditional score, and averaging over the posterior turns it into the marginal score. With the matched schedule αs = bT−s, σs = aT−s the flow-matching ODE is a probability flow ODE on (0, T).

vt(x) = (ḃt / bt) x + at² (ḃt/bt − ȧt/at) ∇ log pt(x)

And the marginal velocity is a marginal score · Flow-based notes, PDF p. 64 · identification on the open interval p. 69, 74.

  • The start is no longer an approximation: the interpolant reaches the prior exactly at t = 0 because b₀ = 0. The price is paid by the matched diffusion, whose coefficients diverge at that endpoint; a diffusion cannot reach N(0, I) in finite time.
  • The field error becomes a velocity error. Conditional flow matching regresses on ȧtx₀ + ḃtxT and its minimiser is the marginal velocity, by the same conditional-expectation argument that makes denoising score matching work.
  • The steps are unchanged. Straight conditional paths do not make the marginal trajectories straight; the solver still follows a curve.

Where the correspondence stops

A real optical trap pulls with a force proportional to ∇I for a small particle; here the force is ∇ log pt, which coincides with the linear restoring force of a single Gaussian beam but is a posterior-weighted sum for the array. The light is sharpened on the variance-exploding schedule τ² + t, which is a mathematical choice, not an experimental one. Defocus and drift stand in for the unknown error of a trained network because their effects can be predicted and checked, not because they are typical. The time grid is uniform; non-uniform spacing is a standard remedy for the stiffness in 3 and is left out as outside the lecture material.

Model and sources

Toy: 48 Gaussians, τ = 0.06, site pitch 0.22, forward dx = dw. Closed forms for pt, ∇ log pt, cell masses and the x-marginal; KL by 140 × 140 quadrature; no neural network. Samplers: the η-family with Euler–Maruyama for η > 0 and Euler or Heun for η = 0, seeded so every run replays. Tests (node --test) check the score against finite differences, the heat equation, the KL bound, cell masses against Monte Carlo, and marginal alignment of the family. Lecture notes: Mathematical Principles of Diffusion Models, Prof. Taesup Moon, Fall 2026 (Score SDE framework; Flow-based perspective; Differential-equations appendix), following Lai, Song, Kim, Mitsufuji and Ermon, The Principles of Diffusion Models, arXiv:2510.21890. Optical trapping: A. Ashkin et al., Observation of a single-beam gradient force optical trap for dielectric particles, Optics Letters 1986.
